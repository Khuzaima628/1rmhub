Nothing here is a secret. These are the points where research agrees most, written so you can
apply them to your bench press, squat, deadlift or any other lift.

## 1. Lift heavy weights

Light and heavy weights can both build muscle, but they do not build **maximal strength** equally.
A 2017 meta-analysis found that muscle growth was similar with light loads (60% of 1RM or less) and
heavy loads (above 60%), while gains in maximal strength were greater with heavy loads
(Schoenfeld et al., 2017).

For maximal strength, the NSCA’s textbook guideline is loads of 85% of 1RM or more, for up
to 6 reps per set (Haff and Triplett, 2016). Lighter sets still have a place, but include heavy
work on the lift you want to max. Use the [1RM percentage calculator](/1rm-percentage-calculator/) to turn
your max into loadable working weights.

## 2. Practise the exact lift

Strength is specific. The American College of Sports Medicine lists **specificity** as one of the
main principles of progress: you get better at what you train (ACSM, 2009). A stronger leg press
helps your squat a little; squatting helps your squat most.

If you want a bigger bench press max, bench press regularly, with the same grip, pause and setup
you plan to use when you test.

## 3. Do enough hard sets each week

In a 2017 meta-analysis, a moderate number of sets per week produced slightly greater strength
gains than a low number (Ralston et al., 2017). More sets help up to a point, as long as you can
recover from them.

A simple way to apply this: if your lift has stalled and you do only one or two hard sets of it a
week, add sets before you change anything else.

## 4. Train the lift at least twice a week

A 2018 meta-analysis found larger strength gains with higher training frequency — but when total
weekly volume was the same, the difference disappeared (Grgic et al., 2018). Frequency helps mainly
because it makes it easier to fit in more good-quality sets.

The 2026 ACSM position stand recommends training all major muscle groups at least twice a week
(ACSM, 2026). Training a lift twice a week also gives you twice the practice of the movement.

## 5. Add weight a little at a time

**Progressive overload** — slowly increasing the demand on your muscles — is the main principle of
progress in strength training (ACSM, 2009). Add the smallest jump your gym allows when a set feels
easier than planned, rather than big jumps that stall quickly.

Many programs use a [training max](/guides/training-max/) about 90% of your 1RM, so the
prescribed weights stay achievable on an average day and progress continues for longer.

## 6. You do not need to fail every set

The 2026 ACSM position stand found that training to momentary muscle failure did not consistently
improve results for healthy adults (ACSM, 2026). Stopping a rep or two short lets you do more good
sets with better technique. The [RPE scale](/guides/rpe-scale/) is a simple way to rate how close
to failure you are.

## 7. Sleep and eat enough protein

- **Sleep:** A 2018 review found that several nights in a row of restricted sleep can reduce
  strength in multi-joint lifts such as the squat and bench press (Knowles et al., 2018).
- **Protein:** A 2018 meta-analysis found that protein supplements slightly increased 1RM gains
  from training — about 2.5 kg on average — and that gains in lean mass levelled off at about
  1.6 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day (Morton et al., 2018). The authors note that
  training is a far stronger stimulus than protein.

## How to know it is working

You do not need to test a true max to see progress. Enter a hard set into the
[1RM calculator](/) every few weeks: if the estimated max from a similar set goes up, you are
getting stronger. Compare sets with a similar number of reps, because estimates from very
different rep counts are less comparable.

When you do want a true max, follow a structured protocol: see
[how to test your 1RM safely](/guides/how-to-test-1rm/).

## Common questions

### How fast can I increase my 1RM?

It depends mostly on your training experience. Beginners usually gain strength quickly, while
experienced lifters progress more slowly, so expect gains to slow down over the years. Track your
own trend rather than comparing it with other people.

### Should I test my max often?

Usually not. A true max is tiring and carries more risk than normal training. An estimate from a
hard set of 2–6 reps is enough for most training decisions; save true max tests for the end of a
training block or a competition.

### Do I need supplements to get stronger?

No. Consistent heavy training, enough sets, sleep and an adequate diet do most of the work.
Protein supplements are a convenient way to reach your protein target, not a requirement.
