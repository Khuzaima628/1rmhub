## What does 1RM mean?

"RM" means *repetition maximum* — the most weight you can lift for a given number of reps. The
number in front tells you how many reps:

- **1RM** — the most you can lift **once**.
- **5RM** — the most you can lift for **five** reps in a row.
- **10RM** — the most you can lift for **ten** reps in a row.

A 1RM always belongs to **one exercise**. Your squat 1RM, bench press 1RM and deadlift 1RM are
three different numbers. It also depends on how you lift: a paused bench press, a deeper squat or
lifting without a belt can each change the result.

## Why lifters use their 1RM

- **To set training weights.** Many programs prescribe weights as a percentage of your 1RM, such
  as "5 sets of 5 at 75%". Without a 1RM, those percentages mean nothing.
- **To track progress.** A rising 1RM — tested or estimated — is a simple way to see whether you
  are getting stronger.
- **For competition.** In powerlifting, your result is the heaviest single you lift in the squat,
  bench press and deadlift.

## Two ways to find your 1RM

### 1. Test it

You warm up and work up to the heaviest single rep you can complete. This gives a real number,
but it is tiring and carries more risk than normal training, so it needs good technique and a
spotter or safety arms. Follow a structured protocol such as
[how to test your 1RM safely](/guides/how-to-test-1rm/).

### 2. Estimate it

You lift a weight for several hard reps and use a formula to predict your single-rep max. Most
lifters do this, because it uses sets they already do in training. The
[one rep max calculator](/) does it for you from any set of 1–12 reps.

## How to calculate your 1RM from reps

The most widely used formula is **Epley's**:

**1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)**

**Worked example:** you bench press **100 kg for 5 reps**.

1. Divide the reps by 30: 5 ÷ 30 = 0.167
2. Add 1: 1 + 0.167 = 1.167
3. Multiply by the weight: 100 × 1.167 ≈ **116.7 kg**

Other formulas give slightly different answers for the same set. For 100 kg × 5, the six formulas
on this site range from **112.5 kg to 119.0 kg**, with an average of about **116 kg**. That spread
is normal — it is why an estimate is best treated as a range, not an exact number. The
[1RM formula guide](/guides/1rm-formulas/) compares all six.

### Tips for a better estimate

- **Use a set of 3–6 hard reps.** Estimates from lower reps are generally closer to a tested max;
  above about 10 reps the formulas disagree much more.
- **Count only clean reps** — full range of motion, no bouncing, no help from a spotter.
- **Push close to failure.** A set that stopped with several reps left in reserve will
  underestimate your max. If you stopped short on purpose, the
  [RPE calculator](/rpe-calculator/) can account for the reps you left.

## How to use your 1RM in training

Once you have a 1RM, you can turn it into working weights. Using the example above (about 116 kg):

- **80%** of 116 kg is about **92.8 kg** — rounded down to **92.5 kg** so it can be loaded on the bar.
- **70%** of 116 kg is about **81.2 kg** — rounded down to **80 kg**.

The [1RM percentage calculator](/1rm-percentage-calculator/) does this for every percentage and
rounds to weights you can actually load. Some programs, such as 5/3/1, use a slightly lower number
called a [training max](/guides/training-max/) instead of your full 1RM.

## Common questions

### Is a 1RM the same as my max?

Yes. "1RM", "one rep max" and "max" usually mean the same thing in the gym: the heaviest single
rep you can lift.

### Do beginners need to know their 1RM?

Not necessarily. Beginners usually get stronger every week, so a number quickly goes out of date,
and maximal singles need practised technique. Estimating from normal sets is usually enough.

### Is testing a 1RM dangerous?

It carries more risk than lighter training because the weight is maximal and technique can break
down. With a proper warm-up, consistent technique and safety equipment, many lifters test
regularly. If you are unsure whether maximal lifting is safe for you, speak to a qualified
professional first.

### How accurate is an estimated 1RM?

Close enough for planning training, but not exact. Accuracy depends on the exercise, the number
of reps and the lifter, and studies have found that the formulas can be off by several percent.
Treat the estimate as a guide and adjust from how your training actually feels.
