<p class="answer">
  <strong>A training max (TM) is a number set deliberately below your one rep max — usually 90% of
  it — that a program uses to calculate your working weights.</strong> If your 1RM is 300 lb, a 90%
  training max is 270 lb, and "5 reps at 75%" means 75% of 270 lb, not of 300 lb.
</p>

The idea was popularised by Jim Wendler's 5/3/1 program, and many strength programs now use it.
Training from a slightly lower number keeps your sessions achievable on an average day, so you
can progress steadily for months instead of grinding through weights that are too heavy.

## Training max vs. one rep max

| | One rep max (1RM) | Training max (TM) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The most you can lift once with good form | A planning number, usually 85–90% of your 1RM |
| How you get it | A tested single, or an estimate from a heavy set | Your 1RM × 0.90 (or 0.85) |
| What it's for | Measuring maximal strength | Calculating the weights you train with |
| Should you lift it? | Only when testing | It is not a max — you are not meant to "hit" it |

## How to calculate your training max

1. **Find your 1RM.** Use a recent tested single, or estimate it from a heavy set of 3–6 reps
   with the [one rep max calculator](/).
2. **Multiply by 0.90** (or 0.85 for a more conservative start).
3. **Round down** to a weight you can load. Rounding down keeps every session achievable.
4. **Calculate working weights from the training max**, not from your 1RM. The
   [1RM percentage calculator](/1rm-percentage-calculator/) does this if you enter your training
   max as the "1RM".

## Why use a training max instead of your 1RM?

- **Your strength varies day to day.** Sleep, stress and fatigue change what you can lift. A
  training max leaves a margin, so the planned weights still work on an off day.
- **1RM estimates are not exact.** Formulas disagree by several percent even on a good 5-rep set,
  and a single tested max can be a lucky or unlucky day. Percentage-based training depends on that
  number being right; a study comparing loading methods notes that an unrepresentative 1RM test
  leads to training loads that are too heavy or too light (Helms et al., 2018).
- **Better technique and fewer failed reps.** Lighter-than-maximal working sets let you practise
  good form and build volume without constantly training at your limit.
- **Room to progress.** Starting lower means the training max can rise steadily for many cycles
  before it catches up with your real strength.

## Should you use 85% or 90%?

**90%** is the most common default and suits most lifters with a reliable 1RM.

**85%** (or lower) is a sensible choice when:

- your 1RM is an **estimate from a higher-rep set** (8–12 reps), which is less accurate;
- you are **returning after a break** or an injury;
- the program has **a lot of volume** or you train many lifts per week;
- your recent sessions have felt like **grinders** at the prescribed weights.

If in doubt, start lower. Increasing a training max that is too low costs you a few easy weeks;
a training max that is too high leads to missed reps and stalled progress.

## How to increase your training max

The common convention, used in Wendler's 5/3/1, is to raise the training max after each training
cycle (usually 3–4 weeks):

| Lift type | Increase per cycle |
|---|---|
| Upper body (bench press, overhead press) | +5 lb (+2.5 kg) |
| Lower body (squat, deadlift) | +10 lb (+5 kg) |

**When not to increase it:** if you miss the prescribed reps, or the last sets turn into grinders,
keep the training max the same for another cycle or lower it. A simple reset is to re-estimate
your 1RM from a fresh heavy set and take 85–90% again.

## Common training max mistakes

- **Using your 1RM as your training max.** The whole point is the margin; without it, the
  heaviest sets become max attempts.
- **Setting it from an all-time best** you hit months ago rather than your current strength.
- **Increasing it every cycle no matter what.** Progress the number only when the work is going well.
- **Rounding up.** Always round the training max and working weights down to loadable plates.
- **Retesting your true max constantly.** Re-estimate from heavy sets instead; see
  [how to test your 1RM safely](/guides/how-to-test-1rm/) for when a real test makes sense.

## Training max FAQ

### Is a training max the same as a 1RM?

No. A 1RM is the most you can lift once. A training max is a lower planning number — usually 90%
of your 1RM — that you never actually need to lift.

### Can I use an estimated 1RM to set my training max?

Yes, and most lifters do. Estimate from a heavy set of 3–6 reps for the best accuracy, and consider
85% instead of 90% if your estimate came from a higher-rep set.

### Should beginners use a training max?

It can help, because it keeps sessions achievable while technique is still developing. Beginners'
strength changes quickly, though, so re-estimate your 1RM every few weeks.

### How often should I update my training max?

Usually after every training cycle of 3–4 weeks, by the small increments above — but only if the
cycle went well. Re-estimate your 1RM every few months to check the training max still makes sense.

### Can I use a training max with RPE training?

They are different ways of choosing load. A training max fixes weights in advance from percentages;
RPE adjusts weights to how hard a set feels on the day. Some lifters combine them — see the
[RPE calculator](/rpe-calculator/) for estimating weights by reps in reserve.
