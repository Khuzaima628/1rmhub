<div class="answer">
  <strong>RPE (rating of perceived exertion) rates how hard a set was on a scale of 1 to 10.</strong>
  In strength training it is tied to <strong>reps in reserve (RIR)</strong> — how many more reps you
  could have done. RPE 10 means no reps left, RPE 9 means one rep left, RPE 8 means two left, and
  so on down to RPE 6 with about four left.
</div>

This reps-in-reserve version of the scale was described by Zourdos and colleagues in 2016. It lets
you train hard without going to failure on every set, and it adjusts your weights to how strong
you are on the day.

**Half steps** such as RPE 8.5 or 9.5 are used when you are unsure between two ratings — for
example, RPE 8.5 means "maybe one more rep, maybe two". Ratings below 6 describe light work such as
warm-ups and are rarely used to plan training.

## How to rate a set

1. **Finish the set, then ask one question:** "How many more clean reps could I have done?"
2. **Turn that number into RPE:** RPE = 10 − reps in reserve. Two reps left is RPE 8.
3. **Count only good reps.** A rep with broken technique, a bounce or help from a spotter does not
   count towards reps in reserve.
4. **Rate straight away.** Your memory of how hard a set felt fades within minutes.
5. **Be honest, not brave.** Rating a hard set as easy will push your next weights too high.

Ratings tend to be more accurate when the set is close to failure. On sets that stop far from
failure, many lifters underestimate how many reps they had left. A review of the research by
Stronger by Science reports that lifters misjudge their reps in reserve by about one rep on
average, so treat each rating as a close guess, not an exact count.

## RPE chart: % of 1RM by reps and RPE

Each cell is the percentage of your one rep max for that many reps at that RPE. It is calculated
with the Epley equation on **reps to failure** (reps done + reps in reserve) — the same method the
[RPE calculator](/rpe-calculator/) uses. A single at RPE 10 is your 1RM, so it is 100%.

## How RPE connects to your 1RM

RPE works in both directions:

- **From a set to a 1RM.** Add your reps in reserve to the reps you did, then estimate your 1RM as
  if you had gone to failure.
- **From a 1RM to a weight.** Choose your target reps and RPE, then find the weight from the chart.

**Worked example:** you squat **100 kg for 5 reps at RPE 8**.

1. Reps in reserve at RPE 8: {rirFromRpe(8)}. Reps to failure: 5 + {rirFromRpe(8)} = **{ex.effectiveReps}**.
2. The six formulas on this site, run on {ex.effectiveReps} reps, estimate a 1RM of
   **{f1(ex.min)}–{f1(ex.max)} kg**, with an average of about **{f1(ex.average)} kg**.
3. For your next set of **3 reps at RPE 8**, the chart gives {pct(rpeFraction(3, 8))} of 1RM:
   {f1(ex.average)} × {pct(rpeFraction(3, 8))} ≈ {f1(next)} kg, rounded down to
   **{loadableDown(next, 2.5)} kg** so it can be loaded on the bar.

The [RPE calculator](/rpe-calculator/) does both steps for you, in kg or lb.

## Why lifters use RPE

- **It adjusts to the day.** If you slept badly, the same RPE gives a lighter weight; on a good
  day it lets you go heavier. A fixed percentage cannot do that.
- **You do not need to test a max.** Hard sets with a rating are enough to keep your estimate
  up to date.
- **It controls fatigue.** Stopping at RPE 7–9 keeps most sets hard but short of failure.

Research comparing the two approaches has found that programs using RPE and programs using fixed
percentages of 1RM can both build strength well (Helms and colleagues, 2018). Many lifters use
both: a percentage to pick a starting weight, and RPE to adjust it.

## Common questions

### What is RPE 8 in reps in reserve?

RPE 8 means you finished the set with about **two reps in reserve** — two more clean reps were
possible.

### Is RPE the same as RIR?

They describe the same thing from opposite ends. RIR counts the reps you had left; RPE is 10 minus
that number. RPE 9 is 1 RIR, RPE 7 is 3 RIR.

### Is RPE good for beginners?

Beginners often find RPE hard to judge, because they have rarely taken sets close to failure. It
helps to practise rating sets, and occasionally take a set to a safe failure (for example on a
machine or with safeties set) to check your ratings.

### What RPE should I train at?

Most strength programs keep working sets around RPE 7–9, with RPE 10 used sparingly. The right
range depends on your program and goals.
