<div class="answer">
  To test a 1RM safely, warm up with a light set of 5–10 reps, build up through heavier sets
  of 3–5 and then 2–3 reps, and then attempt single reps with 2–4 minutes of rest between them —
  aiming to find your max within three to five attempts. Test only with consistent technique and
  a spotter or safety arms.
</div>

<div class="notice">
  This guide is general information, not medical advice. If you have an injury, a heart or blood
  pressure condition, are pregnant, or are unsure whether maximal lifting is safe for you, talk to
  a qualified healthcare professional first.
</div>

## Should you test your 1RM at all?

Most lifters do not need to. An estimate from a heavy set of 3–6 reps is usually close enough to
plan training, and it avoids the fatigue and risk of a true max — the
[1RM calculator](/) does this from any set of 1–12 reps.

A tested max makes sense if you compete, if your program asks for one, or if you want to check an
estimate. It is usually **not** a good idea if:

- you are new to the lift, or your technique still changes from set to set;
- you are injured, ill, or poorly rested;
- you would be lifting alone without safety arms, pins or a spotter.

## Before you start

- **Pick one lift per session**, at the start of your workout, when you are fresh.
- **Use the same equipment and standards** you want the number to represent — the same bar,
  depth, pause and gear (belt, sleeves, straps or none).
- **Set up safety**: squat and bench inside a rack with the safeties set just below your lowest
  position, or use an experienced spotter who knows the plan.
- **Have a target in mind.** Estimate your max first, so you can plan your warm-ups and opening
  attempt instead of guessing.

## The step-by-step protocol

This sequence follows the approach in the NSCA's *Essentials of Strength Training and
Conditioning*, as summarised by HPRC. The increments are guides: smaller lifters and upper-body
lifts use the lower end.

1. **Light warm-up.** 5–10 reps with a light weight you can lift easily (roughly 40–60% of your
   expected max). Rest about 1 minute.
2. **Heavier warm-up.** 3–5 reps after adding about 10–20 lb (5–10%) for upper-body lifts, or
   30–40 lb (10–20%) for lower-body lifts. Rest about 2 minutes.
3. **Near-maximal set.** 2–3 reps after a similar increase. Rest 2–4 minutes.
4. **First attempt.** Add a similar increase and attempt a single rep. Many lifters choose an
   opener they are confident of making — near their estimated max or slightly below it.
5. **If the rep was successful:** rest 2–4 minutes, add a smaller increase, and attempt again.
6. **If the rep failed:** rest 2–4 minutes, take off about 5–10 lb (2.5–5%) for upper-body lifts
   or 15–20 lb (5–10%) for lower-body lifts, and try once more.

Aim to find your max within **three to five attempts** after warming up. More attempts usually
mean fatigue, not strength, decides the result.

The [warm-up calculator](/warm-up-calculator/) builds the ramp with loadable weights for your bar,
and the [plate calculator](/plate-calculator/) shows what to load for each attempt.

## When to stop

Stop the session — even if you have attempts left — when:

- a rep slows to a grind and your technique changes noticeably;
- you feel pain (not just effort), dizziness, or anything unusual;
- you miss the same weight twice.

A max that required a technique breakdown is not a number to train from.

## After the test

Record the weight, the date and how the lift looked. Use the number to set your training
percentages with the [1RM percentage calculator](/1rm-percentage-calculator/), and leave
several weeks of training before testing again. Between tests, track progress with estimated
maxes from heavy sets — a 5-rep test has been shown to predict 1RM more accurately than higher-rep
tests (Reynolds et al., 2006).
