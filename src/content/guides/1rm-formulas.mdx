## What a 1RM formula does

A one rep max (1RM) formula predicts the heaviest single you could lift from a set you did for
several reps. Every formula takes the weight lifted (*w*) and the reps completed (*r*) and returns
an estimated 1RM. The difference between them is the *shape* of the relationship they assume:

- **Linear** formulas (Epley, O'Conner) add a fixed percentage per rep.
- **Brzycki** rises slowly at first and then steeply as reps climb.
- **Lombardi** is a power curve that flattens out at higher reps.
- **Mayhew** and **Wathan** are exponential curves fitted to measured data.

### What the comparison shows

- **The formulas agree most closely at 5–7 reps**, where the spread is under 6%.
- **They disagree more at 1–3 reps**, mostly because Mayhew is not anchored to a single: for one
  rep it returns about 109% of the weight, so it reads high on very heavy sets. (StrengthCalc
  never estimates from a single — 1 rep returns the weight you lifted.)
- **They diverge most above 10 reps.** At 12 reps the spread is over 12%: Brzycki has become the
  highest estimate and Lombardi the lowest.
- **O'Conner is the most conservative formula from 6 to 10 reps**; Wathan is the most generous
  from 7 to 10.

## Why the formulas disagree

Each equation was developed from a different group of people, a different lift or a different
method. Epley's formula comes from a coaching chart; Mayhew's was fitted to bench-press data
from college men and women; Wathen's appeared in an NSCA textbook. None was designed to be
universal, so none should be expected to fit every lifter on every lift.

Your own muscular endurance matters too. Two lifters with the same 1RM can do very different
numbers of reps at 80% of it — and a formula can only assume an average.

## What research says about accuracy

- In a comparison of seven equations against tested maxes in college students, predictions were
  strongly correlated with real 1RMs, but **every equation significantly underestimated the
  deadlift** (LeSuer et al., 1997). Accuracy, in other words, depends on the lift.
- In a study of 70 adults, predictions from a **5-rep maximum were more accurate than from 10- or
  20-rep maximums** (Reynolds et al., 2006).

Both findings point the same way: estimate from a heavy set of roughly 3–6 reps, and expect
lift-to-lift differences.

## When estimates become less reliable

- **More than about 10 reps.** The formulas diverge and fatigue, not strength, ends the set.
  StrengthCalc warns above 10 reps and shows no number above 12.
- **Reps that were not to (or near) failure.** If you could have done two more, the estimate
  reads low.
- **Inconsistent technique** — bounced, half-range or loosened reps make the set easier than a
  strict single and inflate the estimate.
- **Lifts the formulas were not studied on**, such as rows or isolation exercises.

## Why StrengthCalc shows all six, plus an average

Showing one formula hides how uncertain an estimate is. StrengthCalc shows all six and the
**Average Estimate** — the plain arithmetic mean of the six. The average is a convenient summary
of where the formulas cluster; it is *not* a scientifically validated consensus, and we do not
claim it beats any individual formula. The spread between the lowest and highest result is just
as important: a wide spread is a sign to treat the number cautiously.
