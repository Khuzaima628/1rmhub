## What a 1RM formula does

A one rep max (1RM) formula predicts the heaviest single you could lift from a set you did for
several reps. Every formula takes the weight lifted (*w*) and the reps completed (*r*) and returns
an estimated 1RM. The difference between them is the *shape* of the relationship they assume:

- **Linear** formulas (Epley, O'Conner) add a fixed percentage per rep.
- **Brzycki** rises slowly at first and then steeply as reps climb.
- **Lombardi** is a power curve that flattens out at higher reps.
- **Mayhew** and **Wathan** are exponential curves fitted to measured data.

## Each formula explained

Every example below uses the same set — **100 kg for 5 reps** — so you can compare them directly.

### Epley formula

**{fx.epley.equation}**

The most widely used formula, and the default on many calculators. It adds 1/30 of the weight
(about 3.3%) for every rep. It comes from a coaching chart by Boyd Epley (1985), not a
peer-reviewed study. 100 kg × 5 → **{at('epley', 5)} kg**.

### Brzycki formula

**{fx.brzycki.equation}**

Published by Matt Brzycki in 1993. It reads lower than Epley below 10 reps, gives exactly the
same answer at 10 reps, and climbs faster above 10. Because the bottom of the fraction reaches
zero at 37 reps, it is only meant for low-rep sets. 100 kg × 5 → **{at('brzycki', 5)} kg**.

**Epley vs Brzycki:** for a typical set of 3–6 reps, Brzycki gives the lower, more cautious
number and Epley the higher one. At 10 reps they meet ({at('epley', 10)} kg vs {at('brzycki', 10)} kg).

### Lombardi formula

**{fx.lombardi.equation}**

From Vincent Lombardi's 1989 textbook. A power curve: it rises quickly over the first few reps
and then flattens, so it reads high on short sets and becomes the lowest estimate at high reps.
100 kg × 5 → **{at('lombardi', 5)} kg**.

### Mayhew formula

**{fx.mayhew.equation}**

Fitted by Mayhew and colleagues (1992) to bench-press data from college men and women. It is an
exponential curve that is not anchored to a single: for 1 rep it returns about 109% of the
weight, so it reads high on very heavy sets. 100 kg × 5 → **{at('mayhew', 5)} kg**.

### O'Conner formula

**{fx.oconner.equation}**

From O'Conner, Simmons and O'Shea's 1989 textbook. Like Epley it is linear, but it adds only
2.5% per rep, which makes it the most conservative formula from 6 to 10 reps.
100 kg × 5 → **{at('oconner', 5)} kg**.

### Wathan (Wathen) formula

**{fx.wathan.equation}**

Published by Dan Wathen in an NSCA textbook chapter (1994). It is often spelled "Wathan" on
calculators, but it is the same formula. An exponential curve, it is the most generous estimate
from 7 to 10 reps. 100 kg × 5 → **{at('wathan', 5)} kg**.

### What the comparison shows

- **The formulas agree most closely at 5–7 reps**, where the spread is under 6%.
- **They disagree more at 1–3 reps**, mostly because Mayhew is not anchored to a single: for one
  rep it returns about 109% of the weight, so it reads high on very heavy sets. (1RMHub
  never estimates from a single — 1 rep returns the weight you lifted.)
- **They diverge most above 10 reps.** At 12 reps the spread is over 12%: Brzycki has become the
  highest estimate and Lombardi the lowest.
- **O'Conner is the most conservative formula from 6 to 10 reps**; Wathan is the most generous
  from 7 to 10.

## Why the formulas disagree

Each equation was developed from a different group of people, a different lift or a different
method. Epley's formula comes from a coaching chart; Mayhew's was fitted to bench-press data
from college men and women; Wathen's appeared in an NSCA textbook. None was designed to be
universal, so none should be expected to fit every lifter on every lift.

Your own muscular endurance matters too. Two lifters with the same 1RM can do very different
numbers of reps at 80% of it — and a formula can only assume an average.

## What research says about accuracy

- In a comparison of seven equations against tested maxes in college students, predictions were
  strongly correlated with real 1RMs, but **every equation significantly underestimated the
  deadlift** (LeSuer et al., 1997). Accuracy, in other words, depends on the lift.
- In a study of 70 adults, predictions from a **5-rep maximum were more accurate than from 10- or
  20-rep maximums** (Reynolds et al., 2006).

Both findings point the same way: estimate from a heavy set of roughly 3–6 reps, and expect
lift-to-lift differences.

## When estimates become less reliable

- **More than about 10 reps.** The formulas diverge and fatigue, not strength, ends the set.
  1RMHub warns above 10 reps and shows no number above 12.
- **Reps that were not to (or near) failure.** If you could have done two more, the estimate
  reads low.
- **Inconsistent technique** — bounced, half-range or loosened reps make the set easier than a
  strict single and inflate the estimate.
- **Lifts the formulas were not studied on**, such as rows or isolation exercises.

## Why 1RMHub shows all six, plus an average

Showing one formula hides how uncertain an estimate is. 1RMHub shows all six and the
**Average Estimate** — the plain arithmetic mean of the six. The average is a convenient summary
of where the formulas cluster; it is *not* a scientifically validated consensus, and we do not
claim it beats any individual formula. The spread between the lowest and highest result is just
as important: a wide spread is a sign to treat the number cautiously.

Lander's formula, which appears on some calculators, is not included until its equation can be

## 1RM formulas: common questions

### What is the Brzycki equation?

**{fx.brzycki.equation}**, where *w* is the weight lifted and *r* the reps completed. Matt Brzycki
published it in 1993. For 100 kg × 5 it gives {at('brzycki', 5)} kg. It is meant for sets of about
10 reps or fewer.

### What is the Epley formula?

**{fx.epley.equation}**. It adds about 3.3% of the weight for every rep. For 100 kg × 5 it gives
{at('epley', 5)} kg, a little higher than Brzycki below 10 reps.

### Which 1RM formula is most accurate?

No single formula is most accurate for everyone. Studies have found that accuracy changes with
the lift and the number of reps, and every equation tested underestimated the deadlift in one
study (LeSuer et al., 1997). Use a heavy set of about 3–6 reps and look at the range of all six.

### Why do 1RM calculators give different numbers?

Many calculators use just one formula, and the formulas disagree: for 100 kg × 5 they range from
{Math.min(...FORMULAS.map((f) => f.fn(100, 5))).toFixed(1)} to {Math.max(...FORMULAS.map((f) => f.fn(100, 5))).toFixed(1)} kg.
Two calculators using different formulas will give different answers for the same set.

### Can I use these formulas for more than 12 reps?

Not reliably. Above about 10 reps the formulas spread far apart, and a long set is limited by
endurance as much as strength. 1RMHub warns above 10 reps and gives no estimate above 12.
